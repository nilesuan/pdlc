# Phase 02.5 — Solve

**Goal:** Decide *how* to build what Phase 02 scoped, and prove the load-bearing choices with measurement rather than argument, so Phase 03 can draw an architecture around decisions that will hold.

**Duration:** Days to two weeks for an MVP. Longer means the decision list is too long, not that the problem is hard.

**You are done when:** Every decision the solution depends on is either measured against a threshold set beforehand, eliminated by a constraint, or explicitly recorded as reversible and therefore not worth measuring.

---

## What this phase is about

Phase 02 decides what to build. Phase 03 decides the shape of the system around it. Between them sits a question neither one answers: *does the approach actually work?*

That gap is where projects quietly fail. A team picks an extraction library, a queue, a model provider, or a storage engine on the strength of a blog post and a plausible argument, draws an architecture around it, decomposes it into stories, and discovers in week nine that the thing at the centre does not hold. By then the choice is load-bearing in the literal sense: everything rests on it, and replacing it means replacing the parts built on top.

The remedy is not more analysis. It is a small amount of measurement, early, on the few choices that would be expensive to undo.

Three ideas carry this phase:

- **An argued decision is not a proven one.** An ADR with a well-written Alternatives section is still an opinion if nobody ran anything. Desk research tells you what worked for someone else, on their data, at their scale, under their constraints. The gap between "the survey says this is popular" and "this holds our workload at our target" is the entire decision.
- **Most decisions do not deserve this.** The discipline below is expensive and it is meant to be applied to a handful of choices. A register with twenty load-bearing decisions has misclassified most of them. Nygard's bar — a choice that is expensive to reverse, that other parts of the system will depend on, or that constrains future options — is the filter, and it excludes the large majority of what a team decides in a week.
- **The cheapest evidence first.** Constraints are boolean and free to check. A licence term, a data-residency rule, a compliance regime, or a hard cost ceiling can eliminate a candidate in an afternoon. Benchmarking something a constraint already disqualified is pure waste, and it is the most common way this phase burns a week.

The output is a solution plan whose every load-bearing component points at the record that chose it and the gate that holds it.

---

## Who does what

Phase 02.5 is engineering-owned. It has no design or product deliverable of its own, though it frequently sends work back to both.

- **Engineering (lean: Seat 3, the Staff Engineer).** Owns the phase. This is the same seat that already owns architecture decision records and tech-stack picks in Phase 03; this phase formalises that responsibility and moves the technology half of it earlier. Writes the constraints, classifies the decisions, runs or delegates the spikes, authors the selection ADRs.
- **Engineering (lean: Seat 4).** Builds the bake-off harnesses and promotes them into the test suite. The harness outlives the spike and becomes a regression gate, so it is written to the standard of test code, not throwaway code.
- **Product (lean: Seat 1).** Consulted, not accountable. When a constraint eliminates the whole candidate space, the decision that follows is a scope decision, and it belongs to Product. This is the phase's most valuable escalation and it should be loud when it happens.
- **Security.** Involved at the start, not the end. Residency, licence, and regulated-data constraints eliminate candidates before benchmarking. Discovering one afterwards invalidates the spike.

At full-team scale the platform or enabling team typically runs this phase for choices that cross stream boundaries, and a stream-aligned team runs it for choices contained within its own service.

---

## Inputs

- **Problem statement** from Phase 01, or a written brief when the problem is purely technical and no product discovery preceded it.
- **MVP scope** from Phase 02, including what is explicitly out of scope. This bounds the decision list; a decision the MVP does not depend on is not decided now.
- **A representative workload.** The real corpus, traffic shape, or data the system will face. Without this the phase cannot run, because a bake-off on unrepresentative input produces a number that decides nothing.
- **Known constraints**, or an explicit statement that none are known. "None" is a claim and gets recorded as one, because it is usually wrong and it is cheap to check.

## The steps

### 1. Write the constraints before you list candidates

Order matters here, and the ordering is the point. Once a candidate list exists, the constraints get written to justify it.

Cover, or explicitly mark not-applicable: data residency and processing location, licence compatibility, compliance regime, regulated-data handling, hard cost ceiling, existing platform commitment, team capability, and any contractual obligation.

For anything touching personal or regulated data, residency is written first, before any candidate exists. It routinely eliminates whole categories of hosted service, and it is the constraint most often discovered late and most expensive to discover late.

Date the file. A constraints file dated after the shortlist is evidence the ordering was violated.

### 2. List every decision and classify it

One line per decision, with a class:

- **Load-bearing** — expensive to reverse, depended on, or constraining. Gets an ADR and, by default, a spike.
- **Consequential but reversible** — a wrong answer costs days. Gets an ADR, desk evidence is enough, no spike.
- **Routine** — refactor when needed, nothing depends on it. Gets nothing. Decide it in code review.

Justify each classification by naming what reversing it costs. "Load-bearing because it is important" is not a classification; "load-bearing because six services will read this schema and migrating it means a coordinated deploy" is.

Escalating a routine decision to a bake-off is a real failure mode, not merely harmless caution. It burns the phase's credibility and its budget on choices that did not need it.

### 3. Eliminate on constraints

Walk each load-bearing decision's candidate space against the constraints. Record every elimination with the constraint that caused it.

This is the highest-value step in the phase and it costs hours. Most candidates die here. What survives should be two to four options per decision — fewer than two is not a decision, more than four means the constraints were not applied properly.

### 4. Desk-research the survivors

Gather cited evidence on what is left. The workspace's [`../techstacks/`](../techstacks/) survey is the first stop, and its [`../techstacks/00-methodology.md`](../techstacks/00-methodology.md) supplies the source hierarchy: surveys with disclosed methodology and large samples, then official project and vendor documentation, then named-organisation analyst data, then named-author technical writing. Marketing pages and undated blog posts are not load-bearing sources.

Where the survey has no coverage of your domain, that absence is itself informative. It means desk evidence must come from external sources under the workspace's evidence schemas, and it strengthens the case for going straight to measurement.

Desk research narrows and informs. On a load-bearing decision it does not conclude.

### 5. Pre-register the bake-off, then run it

Before writing any spike code, write down: the decision, the candidates, the representative workload, one primary metric with an exact definition, the threshold that decides, guardrail metrics, disqualifiers, a time-box, and an owner.

Then run it, under rules borrowed from experiment design because the failure modes are identical:

- The threshold was declared before results. Moving it afterwards invalidates the run. If the threshold was genuinely wrong, say so, record why, and re-register.
- One primary metric. Secondary metrics inform; deciding on one is hypothesising after the results are known.
- Run to the time-box. Calling it early in favour of the candidate you already preferred is the failure this rule exists to catch.
- Segment the metric by input class. An aggregate that looks acceptable while one whole class fails is the standard way a bad result passes.
- A disqualification is a result. A candidate eliminated in an hour on a licence term is the cheapest outcome available, and it is recorded with the same weight as a benchmark win.
- An abandoned spike is logged with its reason. Otherwise the next person re-runs it.

If the component is probabilistic — a model, an OCR engine, a classifier — the corpus and thresholds built here are the ones it will be governed by for its whole life. Build them accordingly.

### 6. Write the selection ADRs

One per load-bearing decision, in the standard ADR format, with two additions: an Evidence table tagging each claim by tier, and a Reversal cost stated as a number or a band rather than as "hard".

Alternatives carry their numbers. "Rejected because it was slower" without the measurement is not a comparison, and it is exactly the kind of unfalsifiable claim this phase exists to replace.

Selection ADRs share the single numbered sequence with design ADRs and follow every hard rule that governs them: permanent numbering, immutable once accepted, superseded rather than edited.

### 7. Promote the harness and assemble the plan

The spike code becomes a regression gate in CI. The threshold that chose the technology is the threshold that holds it, so an upgrade that silently breaks it fails the build instead of surprising production. A spike whose code is thrown away has to be re-run to answer "did this regress?", which means it will not be answered.

Then write the solution plan: the chosen approach end to end, each component pointing at the ADR that chose it and the gate that holds it, with every still-open decision listed alongside what it is blocked on. An unlisted open decision resurfaces as rework during Build.

## When this phase says no

A bake-off that clears nothing is a successful phase, not a failed one. If no candidate meets the threshold inside the constraints, the finding is that the problem as scoped is not solvable with what is available, and it routes back:

- To **Phase 02** to rescope, relax a target, or narrow the input space.
- To **Phase 01** if the problem itself was mis-picked.

Discovering this at Phase 02.5 costs a week. Discovering it during Build costs the Build.

## Why this works

This phase is a **system extension** rather than a distillation of existing industry research. The workspace's `research/` corpus has no document on technology-selection method, so the prescriptions here are assembled from adjacent verified sources rather than from a single cited practice. That is a weaker grounding than the other chapters and it is stated plainly.

What it does rest on:

- **The load-bearing / routine distinction, ADR format, and the immutability rules** → [`../research/03-design/adrs.md`](../research/03-design/adrs.md) and [`../research/03-design/system-architecture.md`](../research/03-design/system-architecture.md) (Nygard 2011; adr.github.io). Nygard's definition of an architecturally significant decision is the filter this phase uses to keep its own scope small.
- **Pre-registration, one primary metric, no peeking, and hypothesising after results are known** → [`../standards/frameworks/EXPERIMENTATION.md`](../standards/frameworks/EXPERIMENTATION.md), which grounds these in [`08-evolve-processes.md`](08-evolve-processes.md) §4 pitfalls. The claim carried across is narrow and defensible: the failure modes of an uncontrolled comparison are the same whether the thing being compared is a UI variant or a parsing library.
- **Cheapest evidence that actually proves the assumption** → [`../research/01-ideation/discovery.md`](../research/01-ideation/discovery.md) (Lean Startup build-measure-learn; Cagan's four risks). Phase 01 applies this to product assumptions; this phase applies the identical logic to technical ones.
- **Reversal cost as the classification axis** → [`../research/02-planning/prioritization.md`](../research/02-planning/prioritization.md) (Reinertsen, Cost of Delay). The framing that the expensive quantity is the cost of being wrong later, not the cost of deciding now.
- **Source hierarchy and claim tagging for the desk-research step** → [`../techstacks/00-methodology.md`](../techstacks/00-methodology.md).
- **Monolith-first bias and the cost of premature architecture** → [`../research/03-design/system-architecture.md`](../research/03-design/system-architecture.md) (Fowler, MonolithFirst and MicroservicePremium). The same caution that argues against premature architecture argues for proving the components an architecture will rest on before drawing it.

It is assembled here from the experiment-design discipline above. Treat the assembly as this system's opinion rather than as a documented industry standard, and see [`../MAPPING.md`](../MAPPING.md) for the recorded gap.
